Distortion compensation

Distortion compensation allows REW to compensate for the distortion of components along the measurement path. It can be used to reduce the effect of microphone or source distortion on measurements, particularly low cost MEMS and condenser mics which have a dominant 2nd harmonic distortion component.

This feature was inspired by the work of Antonin Novak and Petr Honzík: Linearization of condenser microphones under non-ideal laboratory conditions using a two-speaker setup. How it works

An ideal component passes its input, x, to its output, y, without modification. Ignoring linear gain for convenience, it has a transfer function that is simply:

y = x

Real components alter the signals that pass through them. One model for those changes is to use a polynomial, so the output has additional powers of x. A polynomial model has the advantage of simplicity, but it is memoryless, meaning its output only depends on the current input and not the input history. Real distortions do exhibit memory, but the memoryless model can nonetheless be useful in distortion reduction. Note that the model applies at any signal level, but at very high levels (close to clipping or saturation) the model may no longer be a reasonable approximation.

Let's suppose the transfer function has an additional factor:

y = x * (1 - ax) = x - ax2

That x2 term adds distortion. For a single tone that will be a second harmonic, for two tones f1 and f2 it will be the second harmonic of each tone and components at the sum and difference frequencies f2 + f1 and f2 - f1. We can tackle that by adding our own additional term to counter the distortion:

y = (x - ax2) * (1 + ax) = x - ax2 + ax2 - a2x3 = x - a2x3

The x2 component has been eliminated, but there is now an x3 term. However, since a is much less than 1, a2 is even smaller, so we have traded a larger distortion for a much smaller one. We can also introduce another term to tackle the x3 component, which will in turn introduce a smaller x4 component and so on.

Using the model

The polynomial model suggests a way to tackle distortion. We can measure the distortion components from a test signal, such as a tone or two tones, calculate the polynomial coefficients that would cause those distortions then apply our own compensations by negating those coefficients. However, we quickly encounter shortcomings.

In the polynomial model we can work out what the phases of the distortion components would be, relative to the tone or tones in the input. They turn out to be at ±90° for even components and ±180° or zero for odd components. However, the phases of components in measurements are often not where the model predicts they should be. Other effects along the path shift the phase, so the polynomial can't fully counter them. To deal with that the coefficient must change the phase of the power of x it is countering. That can be done with complex coefficients, so they have a magnitude and a phase instead of just an amplitude. Complex coefficients are implemented in REW by using a Hilbert transform filter to generate a 90° phase shifted version of the power of x they act on. Any required phase shift can be produced by combining the original power of x with the 90° phase shifted version in the correct proportions, which are the sine and cosine of the required phase shift. That can then be multiplied by the coefficient magnitude.

A polynomial with complex coefficients can effectively tackle the distortion contributions for a simple input signal, such as a tone or two tones. However, a further complication then arises. The distortion components can change with frequency, so the correction coefficients are not suitable at different frequencies - in fact they can add rather than reduce distortion if the distortion has changed significantly. To counter that we need a coefficient that changes its magnitude and phase with frequency. That can be achieved by passing the power of x through a filter which has the desired magnitude and phase response. This is referred to as a parallel Hammerstein model. To determine the filter a set of measurements of the distortion components at different frequencies is required. An effective way to obtain that is through a series of intermodulation distortion measurements using a fixed, low f1 and an f2 that varies across the frequency range of interest.

Implementing the correction

There is a further complication to take into account when implementing the correction. Powers of x in the signal increase the signal bandwidth - it doubles for x2, triples for x3 and so on. If we did our calculations at the original sample rate of the data there would be severe aliasing. To deal with that REW oversamples the data, does the calculations at a higher sample rate, then downsamples the data again. The oversampling factor is 4 for 44.1 kHz and 48 kHz data and 2 for 88.2 kHz and 96 kHz data. Data at higher rates is not oversampled, on the assumption that the signals being processed are within the audio band up to 20 kHz or so and the higher powers would remain within the range of those higher sample rates.

Rather than trying to directly calculate the coefficients from the distortion components REW uses ordinary least squares to generate the values. Since the correcting polynomial itself generates further, smaller distortions the least squares process is done iteratively, applying the current correction to the data, calculating coefficients for a polynomial to address the remaining distortions in that corrected data, then composing that polynomial with the previous and repeating.

Making the measurements

Novak and Honzik use a two tone signal with separate loudspeakers for each tone. That measurement approach has the advantage of allowing us to ignore any contribution from the harmonic distortion of the loudspeakers and eliminates their own intermodulation, providing the equipment setup prevents them from intermodulating acoustically - see the paper linked above for setup details. When the REW signal generator is the source of the dual tone signal the separate outputs option routes the tones to separate outputs.

The tone frequencies should be chosen such that the sum and difference frequencies do not fall on harmonics of either tone. Novak and Honzik used 1100 Hz and 1500 Hz. For measuring with REW an alternative approach is recommended, using a low f1 and an f2 that is within the frequency range of interest. The Minimise f2 sidebands option then ensures REW calculates the correction to get the best results around f2. For correcting at a single frequency one measurement is all that's needed. To collect data for a correction filter to apply across a wide frequency range use the RTA's stepped sine measurement mode to make an IMD vs frequency measurement.

If the input device has already had compensations generated and applied it may be useful to then apply compensations to the source that will be used for measurements. A tone at the intended stimulus frequency can be analysed, the compensations will then target the harmonics. Applying that correction to the output should reduce the harmonics significantly.

Note that distortion can vary significantly with temperature, the equipment being measured should be allowed to stabilise at the operating temperature before making measurements.

Applying single-point polynomial compensations

For a polynomial correction around a single frequency set up the signal generator with the desired signal, start the RTA, and make sure the distortion components are being displayed. A 64k FFT is sufficient. After choosing a Compensation order (2 or 3 are suggested) the Calculate coefficients button runs the calculations. The Activity log tracks the progress of the calculations. If a correction is already active there will be a short delay while it is cleared and the input pipeline is allowed to flush through.


The D2 quadratic terms are the 2nd order coefficients of a polynomial that would produce the measured 2nd order intermodulation components. Novak and Honzik apply a 2nd order correction polynomial using the D2 coefficient negated. The Use D2 only button replicates that. d2L and d2H should typically be very similar where the input device frequency response is the same at their frequencies. If they are not the correction will be a compromise.

The Polynomial coefficients of the correction can be immediately applied to the input by clicking Apply correction. The coefficients will then appear in the Applied correction field. Corrections are retained while REW is running and cleared when it shuts down. The coefficients may be manually edited and applied to see the effect of changes in their values. The first coefficient must always be 1. When the distortion compensation panel is opened the coefficients field is initialised to any existing polynomial coefficients being applied to the input.

The Clear correction button removes the correction.

The Oversampling load graph at the bottom of the panel shows how much of each audio data block's time is being used for the distortion correction. It is typically less than 10 percent.

Generating and applying multi-point compensations

A multi-point correction starts by getting a stepped IMD measurement of the system. Here are some example settings, the measurement level will depend on the system but measuring at the highest levels that will later be used will give the best distortion product visibility. The Capture distortion compensation data button must be pressed to get data that can be used for filter generation.


Once the filters have been calculated their response is shown in tabs. The dots are the measurements, the red trace shows the filter response.


If the response doesn't pass through the measurement points there may be an issue with the measurement data, e.g. the distortion components are below the noise floor.

The Apply to input button applies the filter to the current input. Any subsequent data capture will have the correction applied. The correction can be cleared at any time by returning to the dialog and using the Clear correction button.

When input compensations are active the backgrounds of the RTA levels panel and the Measure dialog level history are green. When output compensations are active the background of the signal generator waveform preview is green.

Saving and loading compensations

The Saved compensations tab allows previously generated compensations to be loaded or newly generated compensations to be saved. Compensations are saved as json files in the distortion subfolder of the REW log files folder, whose location is shown on the About REW dialog. There are folders for the input or output device and sample rate with which compensations are saved with names entered when saving them.
